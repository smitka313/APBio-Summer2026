Why Do Biologists Use Standard Deviation?

Biologists use standard deviation to measure how much data vary from the mean. It helps scientists determine whether results are consistent and reliable or whether there is a large amount of variability in the data.

Why Do Biologists Use SEM and Error Bars?

The Standard Error of the Mean (SEM) measures how precisely a sample mean estimates the true population mean. Biologists use SEM and error bars to evaluate the reliability of data and compare groups visually. Smaller error bars usually suggest more consistent data, while overlapping error bars may indicate that differences between groups are not biologically significant.

Investigation 4 - Did the Results Match the Prediction?

Scientists compare observed data to expected results to determine whether differences are likely due to chance.

Watch this video for help: Null Hypothesis and Chi-Square Help Video

Why Do Biologists Use Null Hypotheses?

Biologists use null hypotheses as a starting point for statistical analysis. A null hypothesis assumes that there is no significant difference between the observed data and the expected data, allowing scientists to test whether experimental results are likely due to chance.

Null Hypothesis Examples

Question: Does fertilizer type affect tomato plant growth?

Null Hypothesis: There is no significant difference in tomato plant growth among the different fertilizer treatments.

Question: Does temperature affect bacterial colony growth?

Null Hypothesis: There is no significant difference in bacterial colony growth at the different temperatures tested.

Why Do Biologists Use Chi-Square Analysis?

Biologists use chi-square analysis to determine whether differences between observed data and expected data are likely due to chance or whether the differences may be biologically meaningful. Chi-square is commonly used in genetics to compare observed offspring ratios to predicted Mendelian ratios.

In a genetics cross, the expected ratio is 3 purple-flowered plants : 1 white-flowered plant.

StepStudent Work

2. Subtract expected values from observed values
3. Square each difference
4. Divide each squared difference by the expected value

Assignment #3 - AP Biology Investigation Design

Purpose: AP Biology focuses heavily on scientific thinking and experimental design. Scientists ask questions, identify variables, collect evidence, analyze results, and explain conclusions using biological reasoning.

For this assignment, you will design a biological investigation based on a real-world observation. You do NOT need to conduct the experiment.

Step 1 - Choose a Biological Observation

Choose ONE biological phenomenon that interests you. You may use:

  • one of your photos from Assignment #2
  • an observation from nature
  • an everyday biological process
Possible Topics:
  • plant growth
  • enzyme activity
  • fermentation
  • photosynthesis
  • osmosis
  • decomposition
  • transpiration
  • bacterial growth
  • biodiversity

Choose a topic that can be tested and measured.

Step 2 - Create Your Investigation Design

In Microsoft Word or Google Docs, type your responses using the headings below. Your completed assignment should be approximately 1-2 pages plus a data table. Use complete sentences and scientific vocabulary whenever appropriate.

1. Research Question

Write ONE clear, testable question. A strong research question tests the effect of one variable on another variable, can be measured with data, and is specific and focused.

Strong Example: How does temperature affect the rate of yeast respiration?

Weak Example: Why is yeast important?

2. Background Information

Write ONE paragraph explaining the biological concept involved, why the topic matters, and what biology predicts may happen.

3. Hypothesis

Write an if-then-because hypothesis.

Formula: If ____________, then ____________ because ____________.

Strong Example: If temperature increases, then yeast respiration rate will increase because enzyme activity generally increases with temperature up to an optimal range.

4. Variables and Control

Identify the independent variable, dependent variable, at least THREE controlled variables, and control group. Then explain why the control group is important.

Example: If testing fertilizer concentration: IV = fertilizer concentration; DV = plant height; controlled variables = water amount, light exposure, soil type; control group = plants with no fertilizer.

5. Experimental Procedure

Write a numbered procedure detailed enough that another student could repeat the investigation. Include setup, sample size or number of trials, how measurements would be collected, and how often data would be recorded.

6. Data Table and Graph Plan

Create ONE blank data table with labels, units when appropriate, and clear organization. Then describe what graph type you would create, what belongs on the x-axis, and what belongs on the y-axis.

7. Predicted Results

Explain what you think would happen and why. A strong response makes a prediction, describes expected results or trends, and explains the biology behind the prediction.

8. Limitations and Sources of Error

Identify at least TWO possible limitations or sources of error. Explain how each could affect the results and why scientists try to reduce error in experiments.

9. AP Biology Connection

Identify ONE AP Biology Big Idea and ONE AP Biology Science Practice. Then briefly explain how your investigation connects to each one.
